The default lens on product is in-app. DAU, session length, retention curves, features shipped per quarter. It’s the lens you get when you hire a growth team, when you read most product books, and when you measure success by what the app does, not by what it makes the user do.

The trouble with the in-app lens is that it optimises for time spent inside your product. And time spent inside your product is, for most of the categories we work in, the opposite of what your users actually came for. They didn’t open the meditation app to have a long session. They opened it because they wanted to feel calmer for the rest of the day. They didn’t open the recipe app to scroll for forty minutes. They opened it because they wanted to actually cook dinner.

The relational lens flips it. The app is the means. The change in the user’s life is the product. Did they meditate this morning? Did they actually go for the run? Did they cook the meal? Did they sleep better? That’s the thing they’re paying for. The app is just the path.

This sounds soft until you trace the consequences. When you optimise for the user’s real life, you start cutting features the in-app lens would defend. You stop measuring “session length” and start measuring “did this user adopt the behaviour change.” You design notifications differently — they exist to *prompt* the behaviour, not to drag the user back into the app. You start writing copy that disagrees with the user’s worst instincts rather than confirming them.

And — this is the thing nobody wants to say — your retention curves get strange. Some users will spend less time in the app over time, *because the app is working*. They’ve internalised the practice. They’re meditating without the prompt. They’re cooking without the recipe. Looked at through the in-app lens, you’d call them churn risks and start sending them re-engagement emails. Looked at through the relational lens, they’re your best advocates. They’re the people who tell other people to download the app.

Real-life impact → customer success → retention → LTV. That order matters. If you start with LTV and work backwards, you’ll end up building a product that hostage-takes its users. If you start with real-life impact, the rest follows on a longer time horizon.

This is hard to do in a growth-team meeting. It’s hard to defend to investors who want session-length charts. It’s especially hard if your business model rewards time-in-app rather than outcomes. But it’s what we mean when we say we look at products with a relational lens. It’s the question we ask before we ship any feature: does this make the user’s life better, or does it just make our chart go up?

If the answer is the second one, we kill the feature.
